Methods

This project combines several datasets with different reporting periods, geographic units and definitions. We retained source files, documented transformations and labeled estimates and illustrative elements.

Sources and processing

We worked from archived source files from the Census Bureau, State Department, Department of Homeland Security, ICE, the Deportation Data Project, USCIS and NCES. A manifest records file origins and checksums.

Python scripts reshape the source tables into JSON for the visualizations. The processing records which rows and columns were used and which source release supplied each period.

AI-assisted work

We used Claude Code to assist with data-processing tasks under supervision. We checked transformations against the source files and compared category totals with the corresponding aggregate figures.

One check caught a substitution of a partial-year Census figure with a full-year value from an older release. We restored the original value and made the partial-year label explicit.

Rendering

The visualizations use HTML, CSS and JavaScript with WebGL, Canvas and SVG. Geographic placement uses the source-specific methods described below.

Reading the maps

Dots and routes do not always represent an observed person’s location or journey. Geographic placement may be approximate, and some routes are illustrative. Each visualization’s unit and method should be read alongside its legend.

Methodologies behind the visuals

  • Migration: Census net international migration, years ending 1 July. Data for 2001–2009 come from the 2009 estimates release, 2010–2019 from the 2020 release and 2020–2025 from the 2025 release. 2010 and 2020 cover April–June only. On the US map, one dot = one person.
  • Visas: State Department visas issued. On the globes, 2024, one dot = 50 visas. Arcs route through likely ports of entry and illustrate, but do not record, journeys. The graphs show yearly totals by category for 2001–2025 and every visa class for FY2018–2025, summed from the monthly reports, which agree with the annual totals to within 2%.
  • Green cards: DHS data for the 200 busiest counties plus state totals by country of birth. 99.3% of 2024’s 1.36 million are mapped, one dot per person.
  • Detention: ICE average daily detention by facility, plotted at ZIP code centers, one dot per person held.
  • Removals: ICE removals are those that began with an ICE arrest, by state of arrest and country, counted by calendar year of departure (2022: October–December only). BPS removals follow a border arrest. Their split by Border Patrol sector, and their destination countries on the globe, are estimates.
  • Geography: Census cartographic boundaries and Gazetteer points. County dots sit on census tracts in proportion to foreign-born residents (American Community Survey 2020–2024).

Limitations. Dots are placed approximately around ZIP code, tract and county centers. Visa, green-card and ICE detention data run on fiscal years, ICE removals on calendar years, and the Census counts years ending 1 July.
